# Editing Resources — Camera Angles & Framing

**Purpose:** A reference library of camera angles, viewpoints, shot sizes, and framing choices for cinematic edits, including Roblox / Anime Breaker and faceless Shorts.

**Read this first:** A **camera angle** is the direction and height from which the camera views a subject (low, high, front, rear, Dutch). A **shot size** is how much of the subject appears (wide, medium, close-up). **Composition** is where things are placed in the picture. A **POV** tells us whose viewpoint it represents. These ideas overlap, but they aren't interchangeable. The names below are a wide practical vocabulary, **not all unique industry-standard camera angles**; some are established film terms and others are descriptive placements.

**How to use:** Pick a camera *height* + *facing angle* + *shot size*, then choose optional framing. Example: `low angle + three-quarter front + full shot + centered composition`.

> **Roblox recording note:** To create a real low/high/side angle, record from that camera perspective in-game (using available camera controls, freecam, or another player as camera operator). In CapCut, cropping, scale keyframes, and frame rotation can simulate shot size or a Dutch angle, but **cannot recreate missing 3D perspective** from a single flat recording.

## 01. Camera height & vertical angle (23)

These are camera *angles* in the strict sense: where the camera sits vertically and whether it points up or down.

| Term | What it means | Example use |
|---|---|---|
| **Eye-level** | Camera is about level with the subject's eyes; neutral, direct perspective. | Dialogue, explanations, straightforward gameplay introductions. |
| **Low angle** | Camera sits below the subject and points upward; often adds scale or authority. | Make a secret boss look imposing. |
| **Very low angle** | Steeper low-angle view from close to the ground; exaggerates height. | Towering enemy reveal. |
| **Worm's-eye view** | Camera looks almost straight upward from ground level. | Boss or building towering above the player. |
| **Ground-level angle** | Lens sits just above the floor, often including ground texture. | Show footsteps, weapons, or an approaching enemy. |
| **Foot-level angle** | Camera at foot height; emphasizes feet and the lower environment. | Character arrival or sprint introduction. |
| **Knee-level angle** | Camera near knee height; low without being extreme. | Full-body hero entrance. |
| **Hip-level angle** | Camera near the hip; commonly suits action and holstered weapons. | Character posing with a weapon. |
| **Waist-level angle** | Camera around the waist, often giving a natural mid-body viewpoint. | Show an interaction without an exaggerated perspective. |
| **Chest-level angle** | Camera at chest height; slightly below many faces. | Balanced character showcase. |
| **Shoulder-level angle** | Camera at the subject's shoulder height. | Conversational two-shots or aiming scenes. |
| **Chin-level angle** | Camera just below the face, looking slightly upward. | Heroic facial close-up. |
| **High angle** | Camera looks downward at the subject; may suggest vulnerability or context. | Show how small the player looks next to a boss. |
| **Slight high angle** | Mild downward view; often more readable than a steep top-down view. | Show positioning of a character and nearby items. |
| **Steep high angle** | Strong downward pitch but still reveals the subject's front or side. | Show a trapped character within a room. |
| **Bird's-eye view** | Elevated view looking down over a space; often nearly vertical. | Reveal boss arena layout. |
| **Overhead / top-down** | Lens points straight down toward the scene. | Show pathways, item placement, or floor patterns. |
| **God's-eye view** | Very high, seemingly all-seeing overhead vantage; dramatic overview. | Reveal the entire arena as a tiny world. |
| **Ceiling-level angle** | Camera near the ceiling, looking into the room. | Show characters entering a room. |
| **Underneath / underbelly angle** | Camera looks up at the bottom of a subject or structure. | Emphasize a giant creature, bridge, or platform. |
| **Aerial oblique view** | Elevated view pointed diagonally rather than straight down. | Show an arena and its surroundings together. |
| **Down-the-stairs angle** | Camera above a staircase looking down its direction. | Show an enemy waiting at the bottom. |
| **Up-the-stairs angle** | Camera below a staircase looking upward. | Build anticipation before a boss entrance. |

## 02. Horizontal position & subject-facing angle (17)

These describe which *side* of the subject is visible; left and right are mirrored variants, not different techniques.

| Term | What it means | Example use |
|---|---|---|
| **Head-on / frontal** | Camera faces the subject squarely. | Clean character introductions. |
| **Straight-on profile** | Camera is directly to the side, showing one side of the face. | Side-view combat or movement. |
| **Three-quarter front** | Camera sees both the face/front and one side; flattering sense of depth. | Roblox avatar hero shot. |
| **Quarter-front / slight three-quarter** | Camera barely turns off the front axis. | A subtle, less formal character portrait. |
| **Three-quarter rear** | Camera sees the back plus one side of the subject. | Present a boss facing a player. |
| **Direct rear view** | Camera is directly behind the subject. | Show a player facing a distant challenge. |
| **Back-to-camera** | A character faces away even if camera is not perfectly centered behind. | Create mystery before a reveal. |
| **Side-on / lateral** | Camera views the action from its side rather than front or back. | Clear 2D-like combat read. |
| **Oblique angle** | Camera views the subject diagonally from an off-axis position. | Add depth to a hall or arena. |
| **Diagonal corner view** | Camera placed at the corner of a space, pointing across it. | Fit a wide room into one cinematic shot. |
| **Parallel-to-wall view** | Camera sightline runs along a wall or building face. | Show the length of an alley. |
| **Perpendicular-to-wall view** | Camera looks directly toward a wall or facade. | Display a door, code board, or landmark clearly. |
| **Face-to-face opposition** | Two subjects face each other while the camera views their confrontation. | Player-versus-boss showdown. |
| **Behind-the-subject view** | Camera sees what lies ahead past a subject's body. | Lead viewers toward a secret location. |
| **Reverse angle** | Camera faces the opposite side of an established action or eyeline. | Switch from player looking at boss to boss looking back. |
| **Cross-axis / reverse-side view** | Camera switches to the other side of the action line; can disorient if unmotivated. | Intentionally make a fight feel chaotic. |
| **Axial view** | Camera looks straight down the main axis of a room or object. | Symmetrical corridor or long staircase. |

## 03. Camera roll & horizon orientation (9)

These angles rotate the *whole frame* around the camera's forward axis; unlike a pan or tilt, the horizon becomes slanted.

| Term | What it means | Example use |
|---|---|---|
| **Level / neutral horizon** | Horizon remains horizontal; invisible, natural-looking orientation. | Most guides and readable gameplay. |
| **Dutch angle / canted angle** | Whole frame tilts sideways; creates tension or unease. | A dangerous new boss reveal. |
| **Subtle Dutch angle** | Small frame tilt, enough to feel unsettled without distracting. | Tease something suspicious. |
| **Strong Dutch angle** | Large tilt, emphasizing instability or action. | Big dramatic boss attack. |
| **Left-canted angle** | Frame rolls counterclockwise. | Stylized emphasis, especially for matched cuts. |
| **Right-canted angle** | Frame rolls clockwise. | Contrasting matched angle after a cut. |
| **Horizonless tilted shot** | Subject is rolled without a visible horizon; effect may feel abstract. | Close shot of a weapon, face, or portal. |
| **Ninety-degree sideways view** | Frame rotated a quarter turn; intentionally disorienting. | Surreal or comedic cutaway. |
| **Inverted / upside-down angle** | Camera is rotated 180 degrees. | Dream, nightmare, or upside-down punchline. |

## 04. Point of view & observer position (31)

Who is the camera supposed to *be*? These are viewpoints, some using an angle plus framing.

| Term | What it means | Example use |
|---|---|---|
| **First-person POV** | The scene appears through the character's own eyes. | Show exactly where to walk to find a boss. |
| **Third-person POV** | Camera observes the avatar from outside the avatar. | Most Roblox gameplay showcases. |
| **Over-the-shoulder (OTS)** | Camera looks past a person's shoulder toward a second subject or object. | Player staring down a boss. |
| **Dirty over-the-shoulder** | Foreground shoulder/head is clearly visible and partly blocks the frame. | Make a confrontation feel close. |
| **Clean over-the-shoulder** | Just a small piece of shoulder or none visible; stronger subject clarity. | Keep a boss legible in a short. |
| **Over-the-head view** | Camera looks past the back of a head toward the action. | Player seeing a giant arena. |
| **Over-the-arm view** | Foreground arm leads into the shot toward a target. | Using a weapon or activating a switch. |
| **Over-the-hand view** | A hand enters from the bottom/side, directing attention to something. | Point at a code, reward, or secret object. |
| **Under-the-arm view** | Camera peeks past an extended arm. | Frame the boss past the player's attack. |
| **Subjective POV** | Shot communicates a particular character's personal perception. | A stunned player's view of a giant boss. |
| **Objective viewpoint** | Camera watches action without pretending to be a character. | Clear, informative guide footage. |
| **Observer / spectator POV** | Camera views the action as an uninvolved bystander. | Display a fight while keeping all fighters visible. |
| **Character-eyeline POV** | Camera shows what a character was just looking toward. | Look at a doorway, then cut to what's inside. |
| **Reaction-facing POV** | Camera points at the character receiving surprising information. | Show the avatar reacting to a rare drop. |
| **Hidden-observer POV** | Camera seems to spy on action from a concealed location. | Mysterious boss lurking in an alley. |
| **Peephole POV** | Narrow, circular field of vision as if looking through a door peephole. | Horror or mystery setup. |
| **Keyhole POV** | Camera appears to look through a small keyhole. | Peek at a secret enemy. |
| **Binocular POV** | Tight paired-circle or magnified observing view. | Scout a distant boss location. |
| **Scope / sniper POV** | Camera looks through an aiming scope or target optic. | Identify a distant marker. |
| **Helmet-camera POV** | View fixed near the wearer's head, sometimes with helmet edges visible. | Action or first-person challenge vibe. |
| **Body-camera POV** | Chest-mounted observer angle with partial body visibility. | Immersive gameplay parody. |
| **Security-camera POV** | Fixed, usually high-corner surveillance vantage. | Reveal an enemy entering a room. |
| **CCTV overhead POV** | Surveillance-style top-corner angle, emphasizing monitoring. | Comedic 'caught on camera' moment. |
| **Dashcam POV** | Camera views forward from inside a vehicle. | Driving or racing gameplay. |
| **Windshield POV** | Camera looks through the windshield with interior context. | Travel or reveal across a map. |
| **Rearview-mirror POV** | Camera sees subjects as reflections in a vehicle mirror. | Show what's following behind. |
| **Mirror-shot viewpoint** | Primary subject is viewed in a reflective surface. | Surprise reveal behind the avatar. |
| **Through-the-glass POV** | Scene is observed through a window, possibly with reflections. | Secret room or distant boss introduction. |
| **Screen-within-screen POV** | Action appears on an in-world monitor or display. | Pretend the boss is being watched remotely. |
| **Object POV** | Camera takes the fictional perspective of an object. | 'The loot chest watches you approach' gag. |
| **Inside-object POV** | View originates inside a box, chest, bag, or other enclosed item. | Chest-opening reward reveal. |

## 05. Shot sizes & cropping (not angles) (23)

Shot sizes are included because they are often taught alongside camera angles and are vital to planning faceless shorts.

| Term | What it means | Example use |
|---|---|---|
| **Extreme wide shot (EWS)** | Subject is tiny against a huge environment; establishes scale. | Reveal a massive Anime Breaker area. |
| **Extreme long shot (ELS)** | Very distant framing where the environment dominates. | Show the full route to a boss. |
| **Very wide shot (VWS)** | Broad framing with more subject detail than an extreme wide. | Establish a new world. |
| **Wide shot (WS)** | Shows the full scene or full person with room around them. | Boss standing in the arena. |
| **Long shot (LS)** | Full figure appears from some distance; highlights surrounding context. | Character on a rooftop. |
| **Full shot** | Entire body fits head-to-toe. | Showcase an avatar and its outfit. |
| **Medium-long shot (MLS)** | Often knees-up; balances person and background. | Avatar introducing a challenge. |
| **American shot / cowboy shot** | Typically thighs-up, historically leaving room to see holsters. | Show a Roblox avatar holding a weapon. |
| **Three-quarter shot** | Roughly thighs or knees up, depending on production usage. | Character introduction with some environment. |
| **Medium shot (MS)** | Frames approximately waist-up. | Explain a reward while showing your avatar. |
| **Medium close-up (MCU)** | Roughly chest-up. | A confident or surprised avatar reaction. |
| **Close-up (CU)** | Face or one main object fills much of the frame. | Show a rare item drop. |
| **Tight close-up** | Crops a face or object especially tightly. | Emphasize a reaction. |
| **Big close-up (BCU)** | Even tighter face framing, often forehead to chin. | Strong reaction or humor. |
| **Extreme close-up (ECU)** | Just eyes, a mouth, button, or other tiny detail. | Highlight a hidden code or boss eye. |
| **Detail shot** | Isolates a small, meaningful part of a larger scene. | Zoom into a tiny collectible or sign. |
| **Insert shot** | Close shot of an object or action inserted for information. | Reveal exactly which button to press. |
| **Cutaway shot** | Temporarily switches away from main subject to related detail. | Show the reward screen during gameplay. |
| **Macro shot** | Very close view of small objects, often requiring macro optics. | Dramatic close view of an inventory icon. |
| **Head-and-shoulders shot** | Frames head and shoulders, a common portrait size. | Avatar talking head / reaction shot. |
| **Bust shot** | Framing of head and upper torso. | Present a character or NPC. |
| **Loose close-up** | Face emphasized but includes more headroom/background than a tight CU. | Emotion plus environmental clue. |
| **Environmental portrait** | Character remains prominent while location conveys story. | Hero in front of the boss arena. |

## 06. Number of subjects & relationship framing (17)

These are *shot arrangements*, not changes in camera pitch or height.

| Term | What it means | Example use |
|---|---|---|
| **Single / one-shot** | One character is the principal subject. | Focus on the player preparing to fight. |
| **Clean single** | One character shown without another's shoulder or body in frame. | Uncluttered boss reaction shot. |
| **Dirty single** | One subject is primary, but another is partially present in foreground. | Add tension in conversation or combat. |
| **Two-shot** | Two characters share the frame. | Player and boss together before battle. |
| **Three-shot** | Three characters share the frame. | Show three teammates before a raid. |
| **Four-shot** | Four characters share the frame. | Introduce a four-person squad. |
| **Group shot** | Several subjects appear as a group. | Show multiple bosses or teammates. |
| **Ensemble shot** | Many characters framed as a meaningful collective. | Show a guild ready for an event. |
| **Shot–reverse-shot** | Alternating views of two subjects facing or addressing each other. | Player sees boss; boss sees player. |
| **Eyeline match** | Next shot depicts the direction or subject a character appears to look at. | Avatar looks upward; cut to giant boss. |
| **Matching singles** | Two similarly framed shots show different characters. | Compare player and boss power levels. |
| **Opposing angle pair** | A low angle on one figure contrasts with a high angle on another. | Make one enemy feel stronger. |
| **Foreground–background pairing** | One subject near the lens, the other deeper in space. | Player foreground, boss in distance. |
| **Stacked-depth group** | Several subjects occupy near, middle, and far distances. | Establish multiple enemy waves. |
| **Face-off profile two-shot** | Two figures seen in profile, looking toward each other. | Pre-fight showdown. |
| **Side-by-side two-shot** | Two figures share roughly equal distance from the camera. | Compare two builds or avatars. |
| **Unequal two-shot** | One character dominates more of the frame. | Show boss overwhelming the player. |

## 07. Composition & subject placement (not angles) (24)

These choices strongly affect whether a static shot feels cinematic. They combine with the true angles above.

| Term | What it means | Example use |
|---|---|---|
| **Centered composition** | Subject placed in center for clarity, authority, or symmetry. | Boss standing in a doorway. |
| **Rule-of-thirds composition** | Place key subject on one-third grid intersections/lines. | Position avatar and leave room for boss. |
| **Symmetrical composition** | Balance left/right halves around a visual axis. | Dramatic throne-room introduction. |
| **Asymmetrical composition** | Intentionally unbalanced arrangement. | Create tension before an attack. |
| **Leading-lines framing** | Roads, walls, rails guide the eye to subject. | Guide viewers down the secret boss alley. |
| **Converging-lines framing** | Lines meet at a vanishing point near subject. | Reveal the target at the end of a corridor. |
| **Deep-space composition** | Foreground, middle, and background all matter. | Player, enemy, and portal in one frame. |
| **Flat / planar composition** | Subjects align along a flat-looking plane. | Clean versus comparison shot. |
| **Negative-space composition** | Large empty region emphasizes isolation or anticipation. | Player alone before a massive fight. |
| **Positive-space / frame-filling** | Subject occupies most of the image. | Dramatic close boss portrait. |
| **Headroom-heavy framing** | Extra space left above a head or subject. | Hint that something is above. |
| **Tight-headroom framing** | Very little space above subject's head. | Intense confrontation. |
| **Look-room / lead-room** | Leave space in direction the subject faces or moves. | Avatar looking at faraway boss. |
| **No-look-room framing** | Crop against the direction of gaze; may feel cramped. | Claustrophobic moment. |
| **Balanced composition** | Visual weight feels evenly distributed. | Two equally matched fighters. |
| **Unbalanced composition** | Uneven weight gives unease or directs attention. | Boss looming on one side. |
| **Frame-within-a-frame** | Use windows, doors, or arches to create a second border. | Boss visible through an archway. |
| **Layered composition** | Overlapping elements establish spatial depth. | Foliage, avatar, and arena. |
| **Silhouette framing** | Subject appears as a dark shape against brighter background. | Tease an unidentified boss. |
| **Open composition** | Objects appear to continue beyond frame boundaries. | Suggest a larger busy world. |
| **Closed composition** | Most essential visual elements stay contained inside frame. | Crisp tutorial diagram shot. |
| **Minimalist framing** | Only a few key elements present. | Let one item drop command attention. |
| **Crowded framing** | Many shapes or subjects compete for space; can intentionally signal chaos. | Massive enemy wave. |
| **Visual hierarchy composition** | One primary subject is emphasized over everything else. | Keep the code or reward instantly readable. |

## 10. Lens & projection looks that change perspective (not angles) (14)

These are optics or virtual camera projections. They alter the *look*, even when the physical camera angle stays unchanged.

| Term | What it means | Example use |
|---|---|---|
| **Ultra-wide perspective** | Very broad field of view exaggerates closeness and space. | Make a small room look huge. |
| **Wide-angle close perspective** | Wide lens near a subject enlarges its near features. | Funny exaggerated avatar reaction. |
| **Normal-lens perspective** | Moderately natural-looking proportions and field of view. | Clear, familiar tutorials. |
| **Telephoto / long-lens look** | Narrow field of view makes distances feel visually compressed. | Boss appears much closer to player. |
| **Compressed-perspective shot** | Foreground and background seem stacked together. | Layer boss and landmarks. |
| **Fisheye view** | Extreme wide lens makes straight lines curve. | Chaotic or comedic reaction. |
| **Rectilinear wide view** | Broad view with straight lines preserved, but edge stretching possible. | Establish a large world. |
| **Anamorphic-style view** | Typically wide cinematic composition and distinct lens artifacts; lens look, not an angle. | Cinematic establishing shot. |
| **Isometric projection** | Parallel-ish diagonal view with little/no vanishing perspective. | Overview of a build or game map. |
| **Orthographic projection** | No perspective size falloff; objects do not shrink with depth. | Clean diagram of an arena route. |
| **Axonometric view** | Parallel-projection family, with multiple axes visible. | Strategy map or layout guide. |
| **Top-down orthographic** | Straight-down, perspective-free map view. | Explain a route precisely. |
| **Forced-perspective framing** | Camera placement makes differently sized/distant objects appear related. | Comedic giant item or tiny boss gag. |
| **Tilt-shift / miniature look** | Selective focus or lens geometry gives miniature-world impression. | Make the arena seem like a model. |

## 12. Useful established cinematic shot conventions (14)

These combine angles and shot sizes to create specific storytelling information. They are not camera *moves*.

| Term | What it means | Example use |
|---|---|---|
| **Establishing shot** | Wide view communicates where the action takes place. | Start in Devil City with a landmark. |
| **Re-establishing shot** | Returns to a wide view after close shots to restore geography. | Remind viewers where the player is in the arena. |
| **Master shot** | Complete coverage view of a scene's main action. | Show whole boss fight space. |
| **Reaction shot** | Frame isolates how a subject responds to something. | Avatar reacting to rare drop. |
| **Point-of-view shot** | Displays what a character seems to see. | Cut from avatar gaze to hidden boss. |
| **Reverse shot** | Opposing perspective of preceding subject or eyeline. | Boss 'looks back' at player. |
| **Insert close-up** | Detailed view used to convey key visual information. | Show code text or reward statistic. |
| **Cut-in shot** | Closer view of something within the main action. | Highlight attack meter. |
| **Cutaway reaction** | Brief look to a related side subject or consequence. | Show nearby teammate reacting. |
| **Reveal framing** | Important subject partially hidden until the composition clarifies it. | Tease boss behind doorway. |
| **Hero shot** | Subject framed as iconic, often lower angle and clean background. | Show the player's upgraded character. |
| **Villain shot** | Subject framed to feel threatening, often low, shadowed or imposing. | Secret boss introduction. |
| **Profile confrontation** | Two figures shot in side profile facing one another. | Before boss rematch. |
| **Scale-comparison shot** | Put small and huge subjects together in same frame. | Show how powerful the boss looks. |

## Quick combinations for your faceless Roblox videos

These are **shooting setups**, not transition recipes.

1. **Secret boss reveal:** high-angle wide establishing shot → head-on medium shot of player → low-angle full shot of boss.
2. **Location tutorial:** third-person rear view → high oblique view of the path → doorway-framed shot of the exact entrance → insert close-up of the marker.
3. **Training arc:** full shot of player → extreme close-up of damage number → low-angle hero shot after leveling → scale-comparison shot of player versus boss.
4. **Funny reaction:** normal eye-level medium shot → sudden ultra-wide close perspective on avatar → clean single with lots of awkward negative space.
5. **Codes video:** centered establishing shot of menu → over-the-shoulder view of code interface → insert shot of exact code text. Prioritize legibility over drama.
6. **Tower challenge:** bird's-eye view of floor → face-off profile two-shot → high-angle shot of danger → reaction close-up of avatar.
7. **Before vs. after build comparison:** two matching clean singles at same framing; keep camera angle, distance, and lighting constant so changes are understandable.

## Practical rules

- **Readable before fancy:** Shorts are small screens. The subject should be immediately obvious; reserve extreme angles for a clear payoff.
- **Don't confuse lens with angle:** A fisheye, zoomed crop, or telephoto look doesn't automatically change where the camera is positioned.
- **Keep screen direction consistent:** When cutting between characters, staying on one side of their imaginary 180-degree action line usually maintains orientation.
- **Design for captions:** Leave negative space in safe parts of your 9:16 video, and don't cover codes, boss HP, or locations.
- **Use intentional contrast:** Follow an establishing wide shot with a meaningful close-up; contrast low/high angles when the story calls for a power difference.
